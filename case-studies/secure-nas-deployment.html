Secure NAS Deployment and Network Hardening

Centralized storage, role-based access, network segmentation, snapshots, and layered backup for a small nonprofit association.

Background

A professional association with a small team needed a dependable home for its organizational data. I was working as its part-time IT contractor, responsible for the network, workstations and Google Workspace.

Problem

The organization needed a centralized, recoverable and access-controlled way to store organizational data, and its office and guest wireless traffic needed clearer separation.

My role

I planned, deployed, secured and documented the solution. That covered the storage design, permissions model, backup policy, network segmentation and the written restore procedures.

Requirements

  • One central location for organizational files
  • Named NAS sign-in for office members, with group-based access rather than shared credentials
  • Access based on role, following least privilege
  • Recovery points that make it possible to undo deletions and overwrites
  • A second copy of data outside the office
  • Guest devices kept away from internal resources
  • Restore steps that someone other than me can follow

Solution architecture

Internet traffic enters through the router and firewall, which feeds two separated networks. The trusted office network reaches the NAS. The guest network has internet access only.

The NAS runs RAID storage pools. Shares are exposed over SMB and NFS with permissions assigned to role-based groups rather than individuals.

Security considerations

  • Named office-member access: each office member signs in with an individual NAS account; groups, rather than shared credentials, grant access to the required shares.
  • Segmentation: office and guest wireless traffic are separated so guests cannot reach the NAS.
  • Wi-Fi security: the office network is not advertised publicly where operationally appropriate, but the security control is strong authentication and segmentation, not SSID hiding. WPA3/WPA2-Enterprise is preferred; a unique, randomly generated passphrase of at least 16 characters is used where a PSK is required.
  • Administrative access: management access is restricted, with SSH and VPN configured deliberately rather than left open.
  • Router and firewall hardening: configuration reviewed and tightened as part of the project.
  • Recoverability: snapshots protect against accidental changes; a cloud copy protects against loss of the device.

Google Workspace integration

The recovery design includes a cloud layer for collaboration and off-site redundancy. QNAP Hybrid Backup Sync (HBS) connects the TS-233 to Google Drive or an authorized Google Workspace Shared Drive with OAuth2 rather than a stored password.

  1. Install Hybrid Backup Sync from QTS App Center and authenticate the approved Google Workspace account using OAuth2.
  2. Create a cloud-storage target for Google Drive or a Shared Drive, then choose the NAS folders covered by the backup policy.
  3. Configure an encrypted one-way backup for recovery, or a deliberately scoped sync only where collaboration requires it.
  4. Set a schedule, versioning and retention policy, plus failure notifications so missed jobs are visible to administrators.
  5. Use File Station cloud access and Qsync only for approved team folders, with NAS permissions and Google Workspace sharing kept aligned.

Security note: two-step verification protects the Google Workspace account. Backup encryption, named NAS accounts, and role-based groups protect the NAS side. No credentials, SSIDs, IP addresses, or secret values are published in this case study.

Implementation process

  1. Deployed the NAS and initialized the storage pool with RAID.
  2. Defined the folder structure, named users and role-based groups.
  3. Applied SMB and NFS share permissions per role.
  4. Scheduled snapshots and layered backup policies, including an encrypted Google Workspace cloud copy.
  5. Segmented the office and guest wireless networks and hardened router and firewall settings.
  6. Wrote and stored the restore procedures.

Outcome

The organization gained a structured storage environment with controlled access, scheduled recovery points and documented restoration processes.

Lessons learned

  • Decide the permission groups before creating the shares. It is far easier than untangling them later.
  • A backup is only trustworthy once a restore has been rehearsed and written down.
  • Separating guest traffic early keeps later network changes simple.
